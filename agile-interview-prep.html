Process Fundamentals
3 questions
Deep Dive

Story points measure complexity and uncertainty, not output. The moment they're used for performance reviews or cross-team comparisons, the team starts inflating estimates to protect themselves.

Better alternatives: cycle time (how long a story takes from start to done) and throughput (how many stories shipped per week). These are harder to game and more honest for forecasting.

Reframe the conversation early. When stakeholders start comparing velocity across teams, push back with flow metrics. Use historical cycle time data to forecast deliveries — it's more accurate and removes the perverse incentive to inflate points.

Deep Dive — 3 Anti-Patterns That Expose Theater Agile
  • Velocity as commitment — treating yesterday's weather as a guarantee.
  • Sprint goals that are just lists — no unifying objective, just a backlog dump.
  • Retros with no memory — nothing from last retro is ever referenced again.

A good sprint goal: "Enable customers to complete onboarding without support intervention."
A bad one: "Complete stories 142, 143, 145, 149."

A team doing Agile follows the ceremonies but doesn't internalize the principles — standups are status reports, retros produce no action items, sprints are two-week waterfalls. A team being Agile has psychological safety to surface problems, continuously improves its own process, and treats the sprint as a learning loop.

The Three Non-Negotiables
  • Anonymized input so people speak honestly, not performatively.
  • Pick two max — one or two action items, not a laundry list of twelve.
  • Owner + due date for each item, and reviewed at the top of the next retro.

Without the accountability loop — opening each retro by reviewing last sprint's commitments — retros become venting sessions with no memory. The ritual of saying "we said we'd do X, did we?" is more powerful than any facilitation technique.

Tech Debt & Quality
2 questions
The Debt Ledger Model

Maintain a living backlog item with estimated carrying cost per quarter. Attach numbers: defect escape rate, MTTR, deploy frequency degradation. This gives the PM something concrete to weigh against feature work — and shifts the dynamic from "engineering nagging" to "engineering presenting a trade-off."

Treat tech debt like a product concern, not an engineering complaint. Quantify it in business language — slower delivery, higher defect rates, longer onboarding. Negotiate a standing allocation (typically 20%) and make debt work visible in the backlog alongside features. If leadership won't engage, build refactoring into story estimates so it happens implicitly.

Situation
A monolithic order processing service causing 3–4 hours of debugging per incident — near-zero observability.
Task
Get buy-in for a multi-sprint refactor with no user-visible output.
Action
Ran a 90-day incident retrospective. Calculated ~15 engineering hours/week lost, framed as $180K/year drag. Proposed a phased strangler-fig refactor tied to an upcoming compliance deadline requiring better audit logging.
Result
6-week allocation approved. Incidents on that service dropped 70% within two quarters. Compliance deadline hit on time.
Early Signal Detection Framework
  • Day 1–2 of delay: pair someone on it quietly.
  • Day 3: raise in standup explicitly, offer to descope.
  • Day 4+: PM conversation — replan in the open, don't hide it.

Descoping is not failure. Hiding a miss until the sprint review is.

Situation
Two days before sprint end, a payments integration was blocked by undocumented third-party API behavior.
Task
Decide whether to push toward a likely incomplete delivery or replan.
Action
Called a 20-minute triage. Assessed that even best-case vendor response meant untested integration. Proposed splitting the story: internal wiring + contract tests this sprint, live endpoint next sprint. Presented to PM as a risk-based decision.
Result
PM agreed. Fully tested internal implementation shipped on time. Live endpoint delivered cleanly the following sprint with zero incidents.

Influence & Leadership
2 questions
Cargo-Cult Agile Symptoms
  • Standups running 20+ minutes — no one's blocking, everyone's reporting.
  • Retros produce 12 action items, zero get closed.
  • Velocity treated as a delivery guarantee by PM and leadership.
Situation
Joined a team with all ceremonies, zero outcomes. 25-minute standups, retros with untracked action items, velocity used as a commitment.
Task
Improve process with informal influence only — no direct authority over team operations.
Action
Started with retros: introduced anonymous input, a strict "pick two" action item rule, and opened each retro by reviewing last sprint's commitments. For standups, modeled the behavior — three-sentence updates, blockers surfaced publicly. Brought cycle time data to planning to challenge velocity as a proxy metric.
Result
Within 6 weeks, retro items were closing. Standups averaged 12 minutes. Team was primed for flow metrics because they'd seen the data themselves.
The Shift That's Actually Hard

The move from "big design upfront" to "just enough design" feels uncomfortable — not because people don't understand it intellectually, but because it requires tolerating ambiguity. Acknowledge that discomfort directly. The feedback loops are faster; that's the payoff.

Don't lecture on Agile theory. Pair them early, make the why behind each ceremony explicit, and give them small wins quickly. Make it safe to ask "why do we do it this way" — the best hires push back productively. The ones who ask why the retro format works are the ones who will eventually improve it.

Scaled Agile & Dependencies
2 questions
Key Techniques
  • Dependency matrix during PI/quarterly planning — who needs what from whom, by when.
  • Contract-first API design — publish the interface before implementation so dependent teams can mock and proceed in parallel.
  • Explicit buffer stories — create a story to validate the integration point early in the sprint, not at the end.

Dependencies are a planning problem before they become a delivery problem. Push for dependency mapping during PI Planning. Advocate for async communication patterns so teams aren't blocked waiting on each other. When dependencies are unavoidable, escalate to the program level early rather than letting one team become another's blocker.

Situation
Building a customer dashboard that depended on a data platform team to expose a new aggregation endpoint. Both teams planned producing and consuming work in the same sprint.
Task
Data platform team hit schema complications midway. Our UI work was 80% done with nothing to connect to.
Action
Short-term: built against a mock service so UI work could be reviewed and tested independently. Long-term: facilitated a cross-team retro and established two rules — (1) producing teams must be one sprint ahead of consuming teams, (2) any cross-team dependency requires a signed-off interface contract before either team begins implementation.
Result
Dashboard shipped one sprint late with full test coverage. "Producer leads by one sprint" became a program-wide standard. Zero mid-sprint cross-team blocks in the following quarter.

Principal-Level Meta
1 question
The Through-Line for Principal-Level Answers

Always show systems thinking (changing the process, not just the output), influence without authority, and quantified outcomes. If you can't attach a number to the result, find one.

Situation
Definition of Done was inconsistently applied — stories being called complete without integration tests or documentation, causing rework in QA.
Task
Tighten the DoD without creating the perception of policing the team.
Action
Rather than proposing a policy, brought data to a retro — tracked 8 stories from the last two sprints that came back from QA and mapped them to missing DoD criteria. The team identified the pattern themselves. Then facilitated a working session where they rewrote the DoD. My role was scribe and occasional question-asker, not author.
Result
DoD adopted immediately because the team owned it. QA kickbacks dropped 60% in two sprints. Engineers began self-policing — flagging incomplete stories before they hit review.
